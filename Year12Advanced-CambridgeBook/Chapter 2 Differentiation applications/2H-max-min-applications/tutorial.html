2H Applications of maximisation and minimisation 91 2H Applications of maximisation and minimisation Learning intentions • Solve practical problems about maximising and minimising a quantity. Here are some of many practical applications of maximisation and minimisation. • Maximise the volume of a box built from a rectangular sheet of cardboard. • Minimise the fuel used in a flight. • Maximise the profits from manufacturing and selling an article. • Minimise the amount of metal used in a can of soft drink. Such optimisation problems can be solved using calculus, provided that a function can first be developed for the quantity to be maximised or minimised. Solving maximisation and minimisation problems 17 Maximisation and minimisation problems — also called optimisation Usually a diagram should be drawn. Then: 1 Introduce the two variables from which the function is to be formed. Let y (or whatever) be the quantity that is to be optimised, and let x (or whatever) be the quantity that can be varied. 2 Form a function equation in the two variables, noting any restrictions. 3 Find the global maximum or minimum. 4 Write a careful conclusion. Note: A claim that a stationary point is a maximum or minimum must always be justified by a proper analysis. Example 24 Maximising the volume of a box An open rectangular box is to be made by cutting square corners out of a square piece of cardboard measuring 60 cm × 60 cm, and folding up the sides. What is the maximum volume of the box, and what are its dimensions then? Solution Let V be the volume of the box, and let x be the side lengths of the cut-out squares. where x ≤30, because the side length is only 60 cm. Then the box is x cm high, with base a square of side length 60 −2x, so V = x(60 −2x)2 = 3600x −240x2 + 4x3, where 0 ≤x ≤30. Differentiating, V′ = 3600 −480x + 12x2 = 12(x −30)(x −10), so V′ has zeroes at x = 10 and x = 30, and no discontinuities. x x x x 60 − 2x 60 − 2x Also, V′′ = −480 + 24x, so V′′(10) = −240 < 0 and V′′(30) = 240 > 0. Hence (10, 16 000) is the global maximum in the domain 0 ≤x ≤30, and the maximum volume is 16 000 cm3 when the box is 10 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm.

92 Chapter 2 Curve-sketching using the derivative 2H Example 25 Minimising the surface area of a soft drink can A certain cylindrical soft drink can is required to have a volume of 250 cm3. a Show that the height of the can is 250 πr2 , where r is the base radius. b Show that the total surface area is S = 2πr2 + 500 r . c Show that r = 5 π 1 3 gives a global minimum of S in the domain r > 0. d Show that to minimise the surface area of the can, the diameter of its base should equal its height. Solution a Let the height of the can be h cm. Then volume = πr2h 250 = πr2h h = 250 πr2 . b Each end has area πr2 and the curved side has area 2πrh, so: S = 2πr2 + 2πrh = 2πr2 + 2πr × 250 πr2 = 2πr2 + 500 r , where r > 0. r h c Differentiating, dS dr = 4πr −500 r2 = 4πr3 −500 r2 . To find stationary points, put dS dr = 0 4πr3 = 500 r3 = 125 π r = 5 π 1 3 . Differentiating again, d2S dr2 = 4π + 1000 r3 , which is positive for all r > 0. Hence the stationary point is a global minimum in the domain r > 0. d When r = 5 π 1 3 , h = 250 πr2 = 250 π × π 2 3 25 = 10 π 1 3 = 2r. Hence the minimum surface area occurs when the diameter equals the height.

2H Applications of maximisation and minimisation 93 Cost and time problems There is often an optimum speed at which the costs of running a boat or truck are minimised. • At slow speeds, wages and fixed costs rise. • At high speeds, the costs of fuel and wear rise. If some formula for these costs can be found, calculus can find the best speed. Example 26 Minimising the cost of running a boat The cost C (in dollars per hour) of running a boat depends on the speed v km/h of the boat according to the formula C = 500 + 40v + 5v2. a Show that the total cost for a trip of 100 km is T = 50 000 v + 4000 + 500v. b What speed will minimise the total cost of the trip? Solution a Because time = distance speed , the time for the trip is 100 v hours. Hence the total cost is T = (cost per hour) × (time for the trip) = (500 + 40v + 5v2) × 100 v = 50 000 v + 4000 + 500v, where v > 0. b Differentiating, dT dv = −50 000 v2 + 500 = 500(−100 + v2) v2 = 500(v −10)(v + 10) v2 , so dT dv has a single zero at v = 10 in the domain v > 0, and no discontinuities. Differentiating again, d2T dv2 = 100 000 v3 , which is positive for all v > 0, so v = 10 gives a global minimum in the domain v > 0. Thus a speed of 10 km/h will minimise the cost of the trip.
