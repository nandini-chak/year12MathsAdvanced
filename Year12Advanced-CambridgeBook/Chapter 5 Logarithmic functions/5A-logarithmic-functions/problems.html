1 · Calculator ln values Foundation

Use the calculator’s ln button to approximate, correct to four significant figures. (Remember: \ln means \log_e.)

a \log_e 10 · b \log_e 0.1 · c \ln 123\,456 · d \ln 0.000\,006 · e \log_e 50 · f \log_e 0.02

Quick fact: On the calculator, ln means \log_e (natural log). log usually means base 10. Four significant figures means four non-zero leading digits of accuracy (e.g. 2.303 has four sig figs).

a · Press \ln then 10. Display ≈ 2.302585\ldots.

Answer a: 2.303 (4 s.f.).

Formula: \log_e\!\left(\dfrac{1}{a}\right)=-\log_e a when a>0.

b · Same method · Notice 0.1=\dfrac{1}{10}, so \log_e 0.1=-\log_e 10.

From (a), \log_e 10\approx 2.303, so \log_e 0.1\approx -2.303.

Calculator check: \ln 0.1\approx -2.302585 → rounds to -2.303.

Answer b: -2.303 (4 s.f.).

Method: Same calculator button. Type the full number carefully (no spaces): 123456.

c · Same method · Enter \ln 123456. Display ≈ 11.72365\ldots.

Answer c: 11.72 (4 s.f.).

Quick fact: A number smaller than 1 gives a negative natural log. 0.000006 can be typed as 6\times 10^{-6}.

d · Same method · Enter \ln 0.000006 (or \ln(6\times 10^{-6})). Display ≈ -12.02375\ldots.

Answer d: -12.02 (4 s.f.).

Method: Calculator \ln button. Optional check with product law: \log_e(ab)=\log_e a+\log_e b.

e · Same method · Enter \ln 50. Display ≈ 3.912023\ldots.

Optional: 50=5\times 10 → \ln 50=\ln 5+\ln 10.

Answer e: 3.912 (4 s.f.).

Formula: \log_e\!\left(\dfrac{1}{a}\right)=-\log_e a.

f · Same method · 0.02=\dfrac{1}{50}, so \log_e 0.02=-\log_e 50\approx -3.912.

Answer f: -3.912 (4 s.f.).

a · \log_e(e^3)=3.

Answer a: 3.

b · Same method · k=-1, so \log_e(e^{-1})=-1.

c · Same method · \log_e\!\left(\dfrac{1}{e^2}\right)=\log_e(e^{-2})=-2.

Answer c: -2.

d · Same method · \log_e\sqrt{e}=\log_e(e^{1/2})=\tfrac12.

Answer d: \tfrac12.

Answer e: 5.

f · Same method · e^{\ln 0.05}=0.05.

Answer f: 0.05.

g · Same method · e^{\ln 1}=1.

Answer g: 1.

5 · Change of base Foundation

Use the change-of-base formula to express each logarithm in terms of logs base e. Then approximate correct to four significant figures.

a \log_2 7 · b \log_{10} 25 · c \log_3 0.04

Change-of-base formula: \log_b x=\dfrac{\log_e x}{\log_e b}=\dfrac{\ln x}{\ln b}.

a · Write \log_2 7=\dfrac{\ln 7}{\ln 2}.

Calculator: \ln 7\approx 1.945910, \ln 2\approx 0.693147.

Divide: 1.945910\div 0.693147\approx 2.807354.

Answer a: \dfrac{\ln 7}{\ln 2}\approx 2.807 (4 s.f.).

Same formula: \log_{10} 25=\dfrac{\ln 25}{\ln 10}.

b · Same method · \ln 25\approx 3.218876, \ln 10\approx 2.302585.

Divide: ≈ 1.397940.

Answer b: \dfrac{\ln 25}{\ln 10}\approx 1.398 (4 s.f.).

Same formula: \log_3 0.04=\dfrac{\ln 0.04}{\ln 3}. Expect a negative value because 0.04<1.

c · Same method · \ln 0.04\approx -3.218876, \ln 3\approx 1.098612.

Divide: ≈ -2.930.

Answer c: \dfrac{\ln 0.04}{\ln 3}\approx -2.930 (4 s.f.).

Same method: Expect a negative x because the right-hand side is less than 1.

c · Same method · 7^x=0.7 → x=\dfrac{\log 0.7}{\log 7}\approx -0.1833.

d · Same method · 2^x=0.0123 → x=\dfrac{\log 0.0123}{\log 2}\approx -6.345.

7 · From e^x to \ln x and \ln(-x) Development

a What transformation maps y=e^x to y=\log_e x, and how can this be used to find the gradient of y=\log_e x at its x-intercept?

b What transformation maps y=\log_e x to y=\log_e(-x), and how can this also be read as a dilation?

c Sketch y=\log_e x and y=\log_e(-x) on one set of axes.

Quick fact: Reflecting in the line y=x swaps the coordinates. The graph of the inverse is what you get. y=\log_e x is the inverse of y=e^x.

a · Step 1 · The transformation is a reflection in the line y=x.

Step 2 · That reflection turns y=e^x into y=\log_e x.

Step 3 · A line of gradient 1 reflects to another line of gradient 1. (The mirror y=x is itself at 45°, so “steepness 1” stays “steepness 1”.)

Step 4 · The tangent to y=e^x at the y-intercept (0,1) has gradient 1.

Step 5 · After the swap, that same tangent touches y=\log_e x at (1,0).

Step 6 · So the gradient of y=\log_e x at its x-intercept is also 1.

Answer a: Reflection in y=x. Gradient at (1,0) is 1.

Quick fact: Replacing x by -x reflects the graph in the y-axis. The same replacement is a horizontal dilation with factor -1 (the minus sign flips the graph to the other side).

b · Step 1 · Start with y=\log_e x.

Step 2 · Replace x by -x.

Step 3 · The new equation is y=\log_e(-x).

Step 4 · The inside must be positive: -x>0, so x<0. The graph sits on the left of the y-axis.

Answer b: Reflection in the y-axis, which is also a horizontal dilation with factor -1.

Numbers to put on the axes: \log_e 1=0, so both curves cross the x-axis at 1 and -1. \log_e e=1, so the height is 1 at x=e and at x=-e.

c · Step 1 · y=\log_e x is only drawn for x>0. It crosses the x-axis at 1 and passes through (e,1).

Step 2 · y=\log_e(-x) is the mirror in the y-axis. It crosses at -1 and passes through (-e,1).

ln xln(−x)

Answer c: Both curves, with axis marks at -e, -1, 1, e and height 1.

8 · One change to \ln x Development

Sketch each curve using one transformation of y=\log_e x, and describe that transformation.

a y=\log_e x+1 · b y=\log_e x-2 · c y=\log_e\!\left(\tfrac12 x\right) · d y=\tfrac13\log_e x

Quick fact: y=f(x)+k moves the graph up by k when k>0. Every y-value increases by k. The x-values stay put.

a · Step 1 · The parent y=\log_e x crosses the x-axis at 1, and has height 1 at x=e.

Step 2 · Adding 1 lifts every point up by 1.

Step 3 · The point (1,0) moves to (1,1).

Step 4 · The point (e,1) moves to (e,2).

Answer a: Shift y=\log_e x up 1 unit. Equation y=\log_e x+1.

Same idea: Subtracting 2 moves every point down by 2.

b · Step 1 · Parent points: (1,0) and (e,1).

Step 2 · Down 2: (1,0) moves to (1,-2).

Step 3 · (e,1) moves to (e,-1).

Step 4 · The new x-intercept is where the height is 0 again: \log_e x-2=0, so \log_e x=2, so x=e^2.

Answer b: Shift y=\log_e x down 2 units.

Quick fact: A horizontal dilation with factor k multiplies every x-distance from the y-axis by k. In the equation, replace x by x/k.

c · Step 1 · The equation is y=\log_e\!\left(\tfrac12 x\right)=\log_e\!\left(\dfrac{x}{2}\right).

Step 2 · The parent had x inside the log. Now it has x/2. That is “replace x by x/2”, so the factor is k=2.

Step 3 · Old x-intercept 1 is stretched to 1\times 2=2.

Step 4 · Old point (e,1) moves to (2e,1). The height stays 1; only the x-value doubles.

Answer c: Horizontal stretch of y=\log_e x with factor 2.

Quick fact: y=A\,f(x) multiplies every height by A. Here A=\tfrac13, so the graph becomes one-third as tall. x-values do not move.

d · Step 1 · The number \tfrac13 is in front of the whole log, so this is a vertical dilation with factor \tfrac13.

Step 2 · The x-intercept stays at 1, because one-third of height 0 is still 0.

Step 3 · The old point (e,1) moves to \left(e,\tfrac13\right).

Answer d: Vertical stretch of y=\log_e x with factor \tfrac13.

9 · One change to \ln(-x) Development

Sketch each curve using one transformation of y=\log_e(-x), and describe that transformation.

a y=\log_e(-x)-1 · b y=-\log_e(-x) · c y=3\log_e(-x)

a · Step 1 · Point (-1,0) moves down to (-1,-1).

Step 2 · Point (-e,1) moves down to (-e,0). That is the new x-intercept.

Answer a: Shift y=\log_e(-x) down 1 unit.

b · Step 1 · The x-intercept stays at -1, because the opposite of height 0 is still 0.

Step 2 · The point (-e,1) flips to (-e,-1).

Answer b: Reflect y=\log_e(-x) in the x-axis.

Step 2 · The point (-e,1) moves to (-e,3).

Answer c: Vertical stretch of y=\log_e(-x) with factor 3.

10 · Read the dilation from the graph Development

The graph below is a dilation of y=\log_e(-x). Describe the dilation, and write down the equation of the curve.

Quick fact: A horizontal dilation with factor k multiplies every distance from the y-axis by k. To write the new equation, replace x by x/k. If k=\tfrac12, then x/k=x\div\tfrac12=2x. Dividing by \tfrac12 is the same as multiplying by 2. That is why a factor of \tfrac12 puts a 2 inside the log.

Step 1 · Read the given graph. It crosses the x-axis at -\tfrac12.

Step 2 · The dashed lines show height 1 at x=-\dfrac{e}{2}.

Step 3 · Compare with the parent y=\log_e(-x). That parent crosses the x-axis at -1, because \log_e 1=0 means -x=1.

Step 4 · The parent has height 1 at x=-e, because \log_e e=1 means -x=e.

Step 5 · New intercept -\tfrac12 is half of -1. New height-1 point -\dfrac{e}{2} is half of -e.

Step 6 · Every x-distance from the y-axis has been halved. The dilation factor is k=\tfrac12.

Step 7 · Replace x in the parent by x\div\tfrac12.

Step 8 · x\div\tfrac12=2x. This is why we multiply by 2.

Step 9 · Parent y=\log_e(-x) becomes y=\log_e(-2x).

Step 10 · Check the inside: -2x>0 means x<0. The curve stays on the left of the y-axis.

ln(−x)ln(−2x)

Answer: Horizontal dilation of y=\log_e(-x) with factor \tfrac12. Equation y=\log_e(-2x).

11 · Exponential quadratics Development

a Use the substitution u=2^x to solve 4^x-9\times 2^x+14=0.

b Use a similar substitution to solve 3^{2x}-8\times 3^x-9=0.

c Solve for x:

i 25^x-26\times 5^x+25=0 · ii 9^x-5\times 3^x+4=0

iii 3^{2x}-3^x-20=0 · iv 4^x-3\times 2^{x+1}+2^3=0

Method: Write every power with the same base. 4=2^2, so 4^x=(2^2)^x=(2^x)^2. Then put u=2^x and solve an ordinary quadratic.

a · Step 1 · Start with 4^x-9\times 2^x+14=0.

Step 2 · Write 4^x as (2^2)^x.

Step 3 · (2^2)^x=(2^x)^2.

Step 4 · The equation is (2^x)^2-9\times 2^x+14=0.

Step 5 · Let u=2^x.

Step 6 · Then u^2-9u+14=0.

Step 7 · Look for two numbers that multiply to 14 and add to 9. They are 2 and 7.

Step 8 · (u-2)(u-7)=0.

Step 9 · So u=2 or u=7.

Step 10 · Put 2^x back: 2^x=2 or 2^x=7.

Step 11 · First case: 2^x=2.

Step 12 · 2=2^1, so x=1.

Step 13 · Second case: 2^x=7.

Step 14 · 7 is not a whole-number power of 2, so x=\log_2 7.

Answer a: x=1 or x=\log_2 7.

Same method: 3^{2x}=(3^x)^2. Put u=3^x.

b · Step 1 · Start with 3^{2x}-8\times 3^x-9=0.

Step 2 · 3^{2x}=(3^x)^2.

Step 3 · The equation is (3^x)^2-8\times 3^x-9=0.

Step 4 · Let u=3^x.

Step 5 · Then u^2-8u-9=0.

Step 6 · Two numbers that multiply to -9 and add to -8: 1 and -9.

Step 7 · (u+1)(u-9)=0.

Step 8 · So u=-1 or u=9.

Step 9 · First case: 3^x=-1.

Step 10 · No solution. A power of 3 is never negative, so it cannot equal -1.

Step 11 · Second case: 3^x=9.

Step 12 · 9=3^2, so x=2.

Answer b: x=2.

Same method: 25=5^2, so 25^x=(5^x)^2. Put u=5^x.

13 · Quadratics in \ln x Development

Use a substitution to solve:

a (\log_e x)^2-5\log_e x+4=0 · b (\log_e x)^2=3\log_e x

Method: Put u=\log_e x. Solve for u, then use \log_e x=k means x=e^k. The log is only defined for x>0.

a · Step 1 · Let u=\log_e x.

Step 2 · The equation becomes u^2-5u+4=0.

Step 3 · Two numbers that multiply to 4 and add to 5: 1 and 4.

Step 4 · (u-1)(u-4)=0.

Step 5 · So u=1 or u=4.

Step 6 · First case: \log_e x=1.

Step 7 · That means x=e^1=e.

Step 8 · Second case: \log_e x=4.

Step 9 · That means x=e^4.

Answer a: x=e or x=e^4.

Same method: Put u=\log_e x. Move every term to one side before you factor. Do not divide both sides by u. Dividing would hide the answer u=0.

b · Step 1 · Let u=\log_e x.

Step 2 · The equation is u^2=3u.

Step 3 · Subtract 3u from both sides: u^2-3u=0.

Step 4 · Factor: u(u-3)=0.

Step 5 · So u=0 or u=3.

Step 6 · First case: \log_e x=0.

Step 7 · \log_e x=0 means x=e^0.

Step 8 · e^0=1, so x=1.

Step 9 · Second case: \log_e x=3.

Step 10 · That means x=e^3.

Answer b: x=1 or x=e^3.

14 · Log laws practise Development

a Use the laws for logarithms to simplify:

i \log_e e^e · ii \log_e(\log_e e^e) · iii \log_e\!\big(\log_e(\log_e e^e)\big)

b Express as a single logarithm:

i \ln 5+\ln 4 · ii \ln 30-\log_e 6 · iii \ln 12-\ln 15+\ln 10

Formula: \log_e(e^k)=k for any real k.

a i · Here the power is e itself: \log_e(e^e)=e.

Answer a i: e.

Same formula: First simplify the inside using (a i), then take log again.

a ii · From (a i), inside is e. So \log_e(\log_e e^e)=\log_e e=1.

Answer a ii: 1.

Same method · peel the onion: Use the previous answer as the new inside.

a iii · From (a ii), inside is 1. So \log_e(1)=0.

Answer a iii: 0.

Product law: \ln a+\ln b=\ln(ab) (when a>0, b>0).

b i · \ln 5+\ln 4=\ln(5\times 4)=\ln 20.

Answer b i: \ln 20.

Quotient law: \ln a-\ln b=\ln\!\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right). Note \ln and \log_e are the same.

b ii · \ln 30-\log_e 6=\ln\!\left(\dfrac{30}{6}\right)=\ln 5.

Answer b ii: \ln 5.

Combine laws: \ln a-\ln b+\ln c=\ln\!\left(\dfrac{a\cdot c}{b}\right).

b iii · Step 1 · Plus means multiply inside the log, minus means divide: \ln 12-\ln 15+\ln 10=\ln\!\left(\dfrac{12\times 10}{15}\right).

Step 2 · 12\times 10=120.

Step 3 · 120\div 15=8.

Step 4 · So the single log is \ln 8.

Answer b iii: \ln 8.

15 · Planet distances on linear and log scales Development

Distances of the eight planets from the Sun in astronomical units (AU), with 1 AU = Earth–Sun distance:

Mercury 0.39 · Venus 0.72 · Earth 1 · Mars 1.52 · Jupiter 5.2 · Saturn 9.5 · Uranus 19.2 · Neptune 30.1 (Sun at 0).

a Place the Sun and eight planets on a linear number line. Comment.

b i Place the eight planets on a log scale (omit the Sun). ii Label with actual AU distances. iii Comment on the grouping.

c Give a plausible astronomical reason for the grouping.

d Why has the Sun been excluded from the log-scale graph?

Linear scale: Equal gaps on the line mean equal differences. From 0 to 5 AU is the same picture-distance as from 25 to 30 AU.

a · Step 1 · Draw a number line from 0 to 35. Mark 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30 and 35.

Step 2 · Put the Sun at 0. Then place each planet at its AU number.

Step 3 · Mercury 0.39, Venus 0.72, Earth 1 and Mars 1.52 all sit in the first 2 AU. On a line that runs to 35, those four dots land almost on top of each other.

Step 4 · Jupiter 5.2, Saturn 9.5, Uranus 19.2 and Neptune 30.1 are spread across the rest of the line.

Comment: The four inner planets bunch next to the Sun. The four outer planets are widely spaced.

Log scale: We do not plot the AU number itself. We plot the logarithm of the AU. Equal gaps then mean equal ratios (times by the same amount), not equal differences. Any base works. This solution uses \ln (log base e). Leave the Sun out: its distance is 0, and a log of 0 does not exist (see part d).

b i · Step 1 · Pick Mercury and do it slowly. Distance = 0.39 AU.

Step 2 · Press \ln 0.39 on the calculator. The display is about -0.94.

Step 3 · What that means: e^{-0.94}\approx 0.39. The log is negative because 0.39 is less than 1 (closer to the Sun than Earth is).

Step 4 · Same button for every other planet. Only the AU number changes.

Step 5 · Venus: \ln 0.72\approx -0.33.

Step 6 · Earth: \ln 1=0, because e^0=1.

Step 7 · Mars: \ln 1.52\approx 0.42.

Step 8 · Jupiter: \ln 5.2\approx 1.65.

Step 9 · Saturn: \ln 9.5\approx 2.25.

Step 10 · Uranus: \ln 19.2\approx 2.95.

Step 11 · Neptune: \ln 30.1\approx 3.40.

Step 12 · Draw a number line from about -1 to about 3.5 and put a dot at each of those eight log values. Do not put the Sun on this line.

What to write under the dots: The dot is placed using the log. The label under the dot is the planet’s name and its real AU distance, not the log number. The tick numbers on the line (−1, 0, 1, 2, 3) are the logs.

b ii · Under Mercury’s dot write “Mercury 0.39 AU”, not “−0.94”. Do the same for every planet.

b iii · Step 1 · On the log line the eight planets are much more evenly spaced than on the linear line.

Step 2 · There is still one obvious gap: between Mars (1.52 AU) and Jupiter (5.2 AU).

Step 3 · That gap splits the planets into two groups. Inner group: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars. Outer group: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.

Comment: The log scale spreads the inner planets apart and pulls the outer planets closer, but the two groups are still visible.

c · The gap between Mars and Jupiter is where the asteroid belt sits. A plausible reason for the two groups is that the inner rocky planets formed differently from the outer gas/ice giants (and that gap marks leftover debris).

Answer c: Asteroid belt / different formation of inner vs outer planets (any sensible astronomy reason is fine).

Quick fact: \log of a number asks “what power gives this number?”. 10^n and e^n are always positive. They never equal 0, and they never equal a negative number. So \log 0 does not exist.

d · Step 1 · The Sun’s distance in the table is 0 AU.

Step 2 · A log scale needs \log(0). There is no power that equals 0.

Step 3 · Try numbers that get closer to 0, to see what the log does.

Step 4 · \log_{10}(0.1)=-1, because 10^{-1}=0.1.

Step 5 · \log_{10}(0.01)=-2, because 10^{-2}=0.01.

Step 6 · \log_{10}(0.001)=-3, because 10^{-3}=0.001.

Step 7 · As the distance slides toward 0, the log slides toward -\infty (more and more negative, with no end).

Step 8 · A number line on a page has a left-hand end. It cannot be stretched all the way to negative infinity.

Step 9 · So the Sun cannot be placed on the log-scale graph. That is why part b says to omit it.

Answer d: The Sun is at 0 AU. \log 0 is not a real number; the log runs off to -\infty.

16 · Richter scale Challenge

On the Richter scale, each increase of 1 means the quake is 10 times more intense (base-10 log scale).

a By what factor is a magnitude-6.5 quake more intense than a magnitude-4 quake? (Nearest integer.)

b An earthquake measured 6. An aftershock had 25% of that magnitude. What did the aftershock measure? (1 d.p.)

How the Richter scale works: It is a base-10 log scale of intensity. Going up by 1 multiplies the intensity by 10. Going up by 2 multiplies by 10^2=100. Going up by d multiplies by 10^d. The formula is: intensity factor =10^{(\text{bigger reading}-\text{smaller reading})}.

a · Step 1 · The two readings are 6.5 and 4.

Step 2 · Difference: 6.5-4=2.5.

Step 3 · The 6.5 quake is 2.5 steps higher on the scale.

Step 4 · Each step of 1 multiplies intensity by 10, so 2.5 steps multiplies by 10^{2.5}.

Step 5 · Split the power: 2.5=2+0.5.

Step 6 · 10^{2.5}=10^2\times 10^{0.5}.

Step 7 · 10^2=100.

Step 8 · 10^{0.5}=\sqrt{10}.

Step 9 · Calculator: \sqrt{10}\approx 3.162.

Step 10 · 100\times 3.162=316.2.

Step 11 · Nearest integer: 316.2 is closer to 316 than to 317.

Answer a: about 316 times more intense.

Read “25% of the magnitude” on a log scale: 25% applies to the intensity (how strong the shaking is). The Richter number 6 is already a logarithm of that intensity. If intensity is multiplied by k, the Richter number changes by \log_{10} k.

b · Step 1 · 25% means \dfrac{25}{100}=0.25.

Step 2 · The aftershock intensity is 0.25 times the original intensity.

Step 3 · We need the change in the Richter number: \log_{10}(0.25).

Step 4 · Calculator: \log_{10}(0.25)\approx -0.602.

Step 5 · The minus sign means the reading goes down.

Step 6 · New reading =6+(-0.602)=5.398.

Step 7 · One decimal place: look at the second decimal digit, which is 9.

Step 8 · 9 is 5 or more, so the first decimal 3 rounds up to 4.

Step 9 · 5.398 rounds to 5.4.

Answer b: 5.4 on the Richter scale.

17 · Decibel (sound) scale Challenge

0 dB = least audible sound. Each +10 dB means 10 times louder (also base-10).

a A sound falls from 45 dB to 38 dB. What percentage of the original loudness remains? (Nearest whole percent.)

b A lawnmower is 80 dB. A concert peak is 500 times louder. How many decibels was that peak? (Nearest dB.)

How decibels work, from the question itself: 0 dB is the least audible sound. 10 dB is 10^1=10 times louder. 20 dB is 10^2=100 times louder. 30 dB is 10^3=1000 times louder. The pattern: loudness compared with 0 dB is 10^{(\text{decibels})/10}. Divide the decibel number by 10 first, then raise 10 to that power.

a · Step 1 · Original sound: 45 dB.

Step 2 · Compared with the least audible sound, its loudness factor is 10^{45/10}.

Step 3 · 45\div 10=4.5, so the factor is 10^{4.5}.

Step 4 · Quieter sound: 38 dB.

Step 5 · Its loudness factor is 10^{38/10}=10^{3.8}.

Step 6 · The percentage we want is quieter ÷ original, then times 100%.

Step 7 · \dfrac{10^{3.8}}{10^{4.5}}=10^{3.8-4.5}.

Step 8 · 3.8-4.5=-0.7.

Step 9 · So the ratio is 10^{-0.7}.

Step 10 · Calculator: 10^{-0.7}\approx 0.1995.

Step 11 · As a percentage: 0.1995\times 100=19.95\%.

Step 12 · Nearest whole percent: 19.95% rounds to 20%.

Answer a: about 20% of the original loudness.

Backwards from part a: If the loudness is multiplied by a factor k, the extra decibels satisfy 10^{(\text{extra})/10}=k. So extra decibels =10\log_{10} k. Then add those extra decibels to the starting reading.

b · Step 1 · The lawnmower is 80 dB.

Step 2 · The concert is 500 times louder, so the factor is k=500.

Step 3 · Extra decibels =10\log_{10} 500.

Step 4 · Write 500 as 5\times 100=5\times 10^2.

Step 5 · \log_{10} 500=\log_{10} 5+\log_{10}(10^2).

Step 6 · \log_{10}(10^2)=2.

Step 7 · Calculator: \log_{10} 5\approx 0.6990.

Step 8 · \log_{10} 500\approx 0.6990+2=2.6990.

Step 9 · Extra decibels =10\times 2.6990=26.990.

Step 10 · Concert reading =80+26.990=106.990.

Step 11 · Nearest whole decibel: 106.990 rounds to 107.

Answer b: 107 dB.

18 · Substitution u=a^x Challenge

Use a substitution of the form u=a^x to solve. Give each solution as a rational number, or approximate to 3 d.p.

a 2^{4x}-7\times 2^{2x}+12=0 · b 100^x-10^x-1=0

c \left(\dfrac15\right)^{2x}-7\left(\dfrac15\right)^x+10=0

Method: 2^{4x}=(2^{2x})^2. Put u=2^{2x}. Also 2^{2x}=(2^2)^x=4^x, which makes the last step easier.

a · Step 1 · Start with 2^{4x}-7\times 2^{2x}+12=0.

Step 2 · Let u=2^{2x}.

Step 3 · Then u^2-7u+12=0.

Step 4 · Two numbers that multiply to 12 and add to 7: 3 and 4.

Step 5 · (u-3)(u-4)=0.

Step 6 · So u=3 or u=4.

Step 7 · Because u=4^x, this is 4^x=3 or 4^x=4.

Step 8 · First case: 4^x=4.

Step 9 · 4=4^1, so x=1.

Step 10 · Second case: 4^x=3.

Step 11 · x=\log_4 3.

Step 12 · Change of base: \log_4 3=\dfrac{\ln 3}{\ln 4}.

Step 13 · Calculator: \dfrac{\ln 3}{\ln 4}\approx 0.792 (3 d.p.).

Answer a: x=1 or x\approx 0.792.

Same method: 100^x=(10^x)^2. Put u=10^x. A power of 10 is always positive, so a negative u is thrown away.

Quadratic formula: For au^2+bu+c=0, u=\dfrac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}. Here a=1, b=-1, c=-1.

b · Step 1 · Start with 100^x-10^x-1=0.

Step 2 · 100^x=(10^x)^2.

Step 3 · Let u=10^x.

Step 4 · Then u^2-u-1=0.

Step 5 · u=\dfrac{-(-1)\pm\sqrt{(-1)^2-4(1)(-1)}}{2}.

Step 6 · u=\dfrac{1\pm\sqrt{1+4}}{2}.

Step 7 · u=\dfrac{1\pm\sqrt{5}}{2}.

Step 8 · \sqrt{5}\approx 2.236, so \dfrac{1-2.236}{2} is negative.

Step 9 · 10^x cannot be negative. Throw away u=\dfrac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}.

Step 10 · Keep u=\dfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}.

Step 11 · 10^x=\dfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}.

Step 12 · x=\log_{10}\!\left(\dfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}\right).

Step 13 · Calculator: x\approx 0.209 (3 d.p.).

Answer b: x\approx 0.209.

Same method: Put u=\left(\dfrac15\right)^x. Useful rewrite: \left(\dfrac15\right)^{-1}=5.

c · Step 1 · Start with \left(\dfrac15\right)^{2x}-7\left(\dfrac15\right)^x+10=0.

Step 2 · Let u=\left(\dfrac15\right)^x.

Step 3 · Then u^2-7u+10=0.

Step 4 · Two numbers that multiply to 10 and add to 7: 2 and 5.

Step 5 · (u-2)(u-5)=0.

Step 6 · So u=2 or u=5.

Step 7 · First case: \left(\dfrac15\right)^x=5.

Step 8 · 5=\left(\dfrac15\right)^{-1}, because \left(\dfrac15\right)^{-1}=5.

Step 9 · So x=-1.

Step 10 · Second case: \left(\dfrac15\right)^x=2.

Step 11 · x=\log_{1/5} 2.

Step 12 · Change of base: x=\dfrac{\ln 2}{\ln(1/5)}.

Step 13 · Calculator: x\approx -0.431 (3 d.p.). The answer is negative because a base smaller than 1 turns the power the other way.

Answer c: x=-1 or x\approx -0.431.

19 · Sequence of transforms of \ln x Challenge

Starting from y=\log_e x:

a Use, and describe, a dilation of y=\log_e x to sketch y=\log_e(2x).

b Then a translation to sketch y=\log_e\!\big(2(x-1)\big). (The book writes \log_e 2(x-1). That means the log of the whole product 2(x-1).)

c Then a dilation to sketch y=\dfrac12\log_e\!\big(2(x-1)\big).

d Then a translation to sketch y=\dfrac12\log_e\!\big(2(x-1)\big)-2.

Quick fact (same as Question 10): A horizontal dilation with factor k multiplies distances from the y-axis by k. In the equation, replace x by x/k. For k=\tfrac12, x/k=2x. That is why a 2 appears inside the log.

a · Step 1 · Parent y=\log_e x crosses the x-axis at 1, because \log_e 1=0.

Step 2 · It has height 1 at x=e, because \log_e e=1.

Step 3 · The new equation has 2x inside the log, so x was replaced by 2x.

Step 4 · 2x=x\div\tfrac12, so the factor is k=\tfrac12.

Step 5 · Halve the old x-values. The intercept 1 moves to \tfrac12.

Step 6 · The point (e,1) moves to \left(\dfrac{e}{2},1\right). Height stays 1.

Step 7 · The vertical asymptote stays the y-axis (x=0).

ln(2x)

Quick fact: Replacing x by (x-1) shifts the graph right by 1. Add 1 to every old x-value. Heights stay the same.

b · Step 1 · Start from the graph in part a: y=\log_e(2x).

Step 2 · The new inside is 2(x-1), so x has been replaced by x-1.

Step 3 · Shift right by 1.

Step 4 · Old intercept \tfrac12 moves to \tfrac12+1=\tfrac32.

Step 5 · Old point \left(\dfrac{e}{2},1\right) moves to \left(\dfrac{e}{2}+1,\,1\right).

Step 6 · Old asymptote x=0 moves to x=1.

Step 7 · Inside must be positive: 2(x-1)>0, so x>1.

ln 2(x−1)

Quick fact: A number in front of the whole log multiplies every height by that number. x-values do not move. Factor \tfrac12 makes the graph half as tall.

c · Step 1 · Start from part b. The point at height 1 is \left(\dfrac{e}{2}+1,\,1\right).

Step 2 · Multiply the height by \tfrac12: 1\times\tfrac12=\tfrac12.

Step 3 · That point is now \left(\dfrac{e}{2}+1,\,\tfrac12\right).

Step 4 · The x-intercept stays at \tfrac32, because half of height 0 is still 0.

Step 5 · The asymptote stays x=1.

½ ln 2(x−1)

Quick fact: Subtracting 2 moves every point down by 2. Subtract 2 from every old height. x-values do not move.

d · Step 1 · Start from part c. One marked point is \left(\dfrac{e}{2}+1,\,\tfrac12\right).

Step 2 · New height: \tfrac12-2=-\tfrac32.

Step 3 · The old x-intercept \left(\tfrac32,\,0\right) drops to \left(\tfrac32,\,-2\right).

Step 4 · The asymptote stays x=1. A vertical shift does not move a vertical asymptote.

20 · Dilation or translation? Challenge

a Interpret the change from y=\log_e x to y=\log_e(5x) as a dilation, then as a translation.

b Interpret the change from y=\log_e x to y=\log_e x+2 as a translation, then as a dilation (hint: write 2 as \log_e e^2).

Two readings of the same graph: Product law \log_e(5x)=\log_e x+\log_e 5 links a horizontal dilation to a vertical shift.

a · As a dilation: Replace x by 5x → horizontal stretch with factor \tfrac15.

As a translation: \log_e(5x)=\log_e x+\log_e 5, so shift y=\log_e x up by \log_e 5.

Answer a: Horizontal dilation factor 1/5, or vertical shift up by \log_e 5.

Same idea the other way: 2=\log_e(e^2), so \log_e x+2=\log_e x+\log_e(e^2)=\log_e(e^2 x).

b · As a translation: Shift y=\log_e x up by 2.

As a dilation: Same graph is y=\log_e(e^2 x) → horizontal stretch with factor e^{-2} (because replace x by e^2 x).

Answer b: Shift up 2, or horizontal dilation factor e^{-2}.
