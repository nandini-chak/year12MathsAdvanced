220 Chapter 5 Logarithmic functions 5B 5B Differentiation involving logarithmic functions Learning intentions • Differentiate loge x. • Develop and use standard forms for differentiating log functions. • Use the product and quotient rules when differentiating with log functions. • Use the log laws to make differentiation easier. Calculus involving the exponential function y = ex requires also the calculus of its inverse function y = loge x. The diagram to the right shows once again the graphs of both curves drawn on the same set of axes — they are reflections of each other in the diagonal line y = x. Using this reflection, the important features of y = loge x are: • The domain is x > 0 and the range is all real x. • The x-intercept is 1, and the gradient there is 1. • The y-axis is a vertical asymptote. • As x →∞, y →∞(look at its reflection y = ex to see this). • In its whole domain, loge x is increasing, but the rate of increase is decreasing. x y 1 2 1 2 e e Differentiating the logarithmic function The logarithmic function = loge x can be differentiated easily using the known derivative of its inverse function ex. Let y = loge x. Then x = ey, by the definition of logarithms. Differentiating, dx dy = ey, because the exponential function is its own derivative, = x, because ey = x, and taking reciprocals, dy dx = 1 x , a trick that is allowed by the chain rule. Hence the derivative of the logarithmic function is the reciprocal function. 3 The derivative of the logarithmic function is the reciprocal function d dx loge x = 1 x The next worked example uses the derivative to confirm that y = loge x has two properties that were already clear from the reflection in the diagram above. Example 9 Using the chain rule with loge x Differentiate each function using the chain rule. (Part b is a standard form.) loge(3x + 4) a loge(ax + b) b loge(x2 + 1) c Solution a Let y = loge(3x + 4). Then dy dx = dy du × du dx (chain rule) = 1 3x + 4 × 3 = 3 3x + 4 . Let u = 3x + 4. Then y = loge u. Hence du dx = 3 and dy du = 1 u . b Let y = loge(ax + b). Then dy dx = dy du × du dx (chain rule) = 1 ax + b × a = a ax + b . Let u = ax + b. Then y = loge u. Hence du dx = a and dy du = 1 u .

Standard forms for differentiation It is convenient to write down two further standard forms for differentiation based on the chain rule, giving three forms altogether. 4 Standard forms for differentiating logarithmic functions d dx loge x = 1 x and d dx loge(ax + b) = a ax + b Some prefer to use a standard form when differentiating with the chain rule: d dx loge u = u′ u OR d dx loge f(x) = f ′(x) f(x) The second of these standard forms was proven in part b of the previous worked example. Part a was an example of it. The third standard form is a more general chain-rule extension — part c of the previous worked example was a good example of it. This standard form will be needed later for integration. To prove the standard form: Let y = loge u, where u is a function of x. Then dy dx = dy du × du dx (chain rule) = 1 u du dx , or it can be written more concisely as dy dx = u′ u . For now, either learn it, in one of its two forms, or apply the chain rule each time. Then y′ = vu′ + uv′ product rule = 3x2 ln x + x3 × 1 x = x2(1 + 3 ln x). Let u = x3 and v = ln x. Then u′ = 3x2 and v′ = 1 x . b Let y = ln(1 + x) x . Using the log laws to make differentiation easier The next worked example shows the use of the log laws to avoid a combination of the chain and quotient rules. Example 12 Using the log laws to make differentiation easier Use the log laws to simplify each expression, then differentiate it. loge 7x2 a loge(3x −7)5 b loge 1 + x 1 −x . c Solution a Let y = loge 7x2. Then y = loge 7 + loge x2 (log of a product is the sum of the logs) = loge 7 + 2 loge x (log of a power is the multiple of the log), so dy dx = 2 x (loge 7 is a constant, with derivative zero).
