Tutorial · theory

1D · Solving problems involving APs and GPs

Three tools: a test for three numbers, two equations when two terms are known, and a logarithm when the unknown is the term number.

T Teacher note
Each equation sits on its own line. The three-term GP example keeps both square roots. The simultaneous examples use the book’s subtract-for-an-AP and divide-for-a-GP pattern.

Theory · key ideas

An AP adds the same gap. A GP multiplies by the same number. This section uses those two facts to find a missing number.

  • Three numbers in an AP. The gaps match: middle − first = third − middle. The middle is the average of the other two.
  • Three non-zero numbers in a GP. The multipliers match: middle ÷ first = third ÷ middle. So (middle)2 = first × third. A square has two roots, so write both, unless the story rules one out.
  • Two known terms of an AP. Write a_n=a+(n-1)d twice. Subtract. The first term a cancels, and you are left with d.
  • Two known terms of a GP. Write a_n=a r^{n-1} twice. Divide the later equation by the earlier one. The first term a cancels, and you are left with a power of r.
  • The unknown is “which term”. You get an equation like 2\times 3^{n-1}>1\,000\,000. Try powers on a calculator, or use n-1=\dfrac{\ln K}{\ln 3}.
m=\dfrac{1}{2}(a+b)\qquad\text{three terms in an AP}
g^{2}=ab\qquad\text{three non-zero terms in a GP}

Worked mini-examples

Example · the middle of an AP

Formula: If a, m, b are in AP, then m-a=b-m. Solving that gives m=\dfrac{1}{2}(a+b).

Step 1 · Find m if 3,\; m,\; 12 is an AP.

Step 2 · Equal gaps: m-3=12-m.

Step 3 · Add m: 2m-3=12.

Step 4 · Add 3: 2m=15.

Step 5 · Divide by 2: m=\dfrac{15}{2}=7\dfrac{1}{2}.

Check · 7\dfrac{1}{2}-3=4\dfrac{1}{2} and 12-7\dfrac{1}{2}=4\dfrac{1}{2}. The gaps match.

Example · the middle of a GP

Formula: If a, g, b are non-zero and in GP, then g^{2}=ab. Both a positive and a negative square root can work.

Step 1 · Find g if 3,\; g,\; 12 is a GP.

Step 2 · g^{2}=3\times 12.

Step 3 · g^{2}=36.

Step 4 · 6^{2}=36 and (-6)^{2}=36, so g=6 or g=-6.

Check · 3,\; 6,\; 12 multiplies by 2. 3,\; -6,\; 12 multiplies by -2. Both are GPs.

Example · two terms of an AP

Formula: a_n=a+(n-1)d. Term 3 has had 2 jumps, so it is a+2d. Term 12 has had 11 jumps, so it is a+11d.

Step 1 · The 3rd term is 16 and the 12th term is 79. Find the 41st term.

Step 2 · a+2d=16. Call this equation (1).

Step 3 · a+11d=79. Call this equation (2).

Step 4 · Subtract (1) from (2). The a cancels. 9d=63, so d=7.

Step 5 · Put d=7 into (1). a+14=16, so a=2.

Step 6 · The 41st term has had 40 jumps. a_{41}=2+40\times 7.

Step 7 · 40\times 7=280, so a_{41}=2+280=282.

Example · two terms of a GP

Formula: a_n=a r^{n-1}. Dividing a later term by an earlier one cancels a. From term 4 to term 7 is 3 multiplications, so \dfrac{a_7}{a_4}=r^{3}.

Step 1 · A GP has 4th term 6 and 7th term 162. Find a and r.

Step 2 · \dfrac{162}{6}=27, so r^{3}=27.

Step 3 · The real cube root of 27 is 3. r=3.

Step 4 · The 4th term uses power 3. a\times 3^{3}=6.

Step 5 · 3^{3}=27, so 27a=6 and a=\dfrac{6}{27}=\dfrac{2}{9}.

Example · which term is the first past a target

Quick fact: If b^{n}=K and b>1, then n=\dfrac{\ln K}{\ln b}. The same line works for “greater than”.

Step 1 · The GP is 2,\; 6,\; 18,\; \ldots. So a=2 and r=3, and a_n=2\times 3^{n-1}.

Step 2 · We want the first term over 1\,000\,000. 2\times 3^{n-1}>1\,000\,000.

Step 3 · Divide by 2. 3^{n-1}>500\,000.

Step 4 · n-1>\dfrac{\ln 500\,000}{\ln 3}\approx 11.94, so n>12.94.

Step 5 · Check the whole numbers. Term 12 uses power 11: 2\times 3^{11}=354\,294, still under. Term 13 uses power 12: 2\times 3^{12}=1\,062\,882, over.

Answer · The first term over 1\,000\,000 is a_{13}=1\,062\,882.
