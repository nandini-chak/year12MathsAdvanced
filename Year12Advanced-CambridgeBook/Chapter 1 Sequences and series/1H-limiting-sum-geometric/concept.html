• Develop a condition for a GP to have a limiting sum, and develop its formula. There is a sad story of a perishing frog, dying of thirst only 8 metres from the edge of a waterhole. He first jumps 4 metres towards it, his second jump is 2 metres, then each successive jump is half the previous jump. Does the frog perish? The jumps form a GP, whose terms an and sums Sn are as follows: an 4 2 1 1 2 1 4 1 8 1 16 · · · Sn 4 6 7 7 1 2 7 3 4 7 7 8 7 15 16 · · · The successive jumps 4, 2, 1, 1 2, 1 4, ... have limit zero, because they are halving each time. It seems too that the successive sums Sn have limit 8, meaning that the frog’s total distance gets ‘as close as we like’ to 8 metres. So provided that the frog can stick his tongue out even the merest fraction of a millimetre, eventually he will get some water to drink and be saved. The limiting sum of a GP We can describe all this more precisely by looking at the sum Sn of the first n terms and examining what happens as n →∞. The series 4 + 2 + 1 + 1 2 + · · · is a GP with a = 4 and r = 1 2. Hence Sn does indeed have limit 8(1 −0) = 8, as the table of values suggested. Suppose also that the ratio r lies in the interval −1 < r < 1. Then as n →∞, the successive powers r1, r2, r3, r4, .. . get smaller and smaller, that is, rn →0 and 1 −rn →1.
