1H The limiting sum of a geometric series 43 1H The limiting sum of a geometric series Learning intentions • Develop a condition for a GP to have a limiting sum, and develop its formula. There is a sad story of a perishing frog, dying of thirst only 8 metres from the edge of a waterhole. He first jumps 4 metres towards it, his second jump is 2 metres, then each successive jump is half the previous jump. Does the frog perish? The jumps form a GP, whose terms an and sums Sn are as follows: an 4 2 1 1 2 1 4 1 8 1 16 · · · Sn 4 6 7 7 1 2 7 3 4 7 7 8 7 15 16 · · · The successive jumps 4, 2, 1, 1 2, 1 4, ... have limit zero, because they are halving each time. It seems too that the successive sums Sn have limit 8, meaning that the frog’s total distance gets ‘as close as we like’ to 8 metres. So provided that the frog can stick his tongue out even the merest fraction of a millimetre, eventually he will get some water to drink and be saved. The limiting sum of a GP We can describe all this more precisely by looking at the sum Sn of the first n terms and examining what happens as n →∞. The series 4 + 2 + 1 + 1 2 + · · · is a GP with a = 4 and r = 1 2. Using the formula for the sum to n terms of the series: Sn = a(1 −rn) 1 −r (using this formula because r < 1) = 4  1 −( 1 2)n 1 −1 2 = 4 ×  1 −( 1 2)n ÷ 1 2 = 8  1 −( 1 2)n . As n increases, the term ( 1 2)n gets closer and closer to zero: ( 1 2)2 = 1 4, ( 1 2)3 = 1 8, ( 1 2)4 = 1 16, ( 1 2)5 = 1 32, ( 1 2)6 = 1 64, . . . so that ( 1 2)n has limit zero as n →∞. Hence Sn does indeed have limit 8(1 −0) = 8, as the table of values suggested. There are several different common notations and words for this situation: 15 Notations for the limiting sum Take as an example the series 4 + 2 + 1 + 1 2 + · · · . • Sn →8 as n →∞. (‘Sn has limit 8 as n increases without bound.’) • limn→∞Sn = 8. (‘The limit of Sn, as n increases without bound, is 8.’) • The series 4 + 2 + 1 + 1 2 + · · · has limiting sum S∞= 8. • The series 4 + 2 + 1 + 1 2 + · · · converges to the limit S∞= 8. • 4 + 2 + 1 + 1 2 + · · · = 8. The usual symbol for the limiting sum is S∞.

44 Chapter 1 Sequences and series 1H The general case Suppose now that an is a GP with first term a and ratio r, so that: an = arn−1 and Sn = a(1 −rn) 1 −r . Suppose also that the ratio r lies in the interval −1 < r < 1. Then as n →∞, the successive powers r1, r2, r3, r4, .. . get smaller and smaller, that is, rn →0 and 1 −rn →1. Thus both the nth term an and the sum Sn converge to a limit: lim n→∞an = lim n→∞arn−1 = 0 and lim n→∞Sn = lim n→∞ a(1 −rn) 1 −r = a 1 −r . 16 The limiting sum of a geometric series • Suppose that |r| < 1, that is, −1 < r < 1. Then rn →0 as n →∞, so the terms and the partial sums of the GP both converge to a limit: lim n→∞an = 0 and S∞= lim n→∞Sn = a 1 −r . • If |r| ≥1, then the partial sums Sn do not converge to a limit. Example 34 Solving a GP problem involving a limiting sum Find the ratio of a GP whose first term is 10 and whose limiting sum is 40. Solution We know that S∞= 40. Using the formula, a 1 −r = 40, and substituting a = 10 gives 10 1 −r = 40 10 = 40(1 −r) 1 = 4 −4r 4r = 3 r = 3 4. Sigma notation for infinite sums When −1 < r < 1 and the GP converges, the limiting sum S∞can also be written as an infinite sum, either using sigma notation or dots: ∞ X n=1 arn−1 = a 1 −r or a + ar + ar2 + · · · = a 1 −r , and we say that the series P∞ n=1 arn−1 = a + ar + ar2 + · · · converges to a 1 −r . Note: It is not possible to add up an infinite number of terms — addition is a procedure with finitely many steps. The objects above are not really sums at all, but are limits of finite sums as the number of terms increases without bound.
