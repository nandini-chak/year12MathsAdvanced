Tutorial · theory

1H · The limiting sum of a geometric series

If each new term shrinks towards zero, the running total settles on one number. That number is the limiting sum.

T Teacher note
The test is -1<r<1, written |r|<1. r=-1 and r=1 do not give a limiting sum. The symbol in the book is S_\infty or just S.

Theory · key ideas

A limiting sum is the number the partial sums get as close to as we like, when we keep adding forever.

  • Picture. A frog is 8 m from water. It jumps 4 m, then 2 m, then 1 m, then half the previous jump each time. The jumps shrink. The totals climb towards 8 m, so the frog does reach the water.
  • The test. The ratio must satisfy |r|<1. Then r^n gets closer and closer to 0.
  • The formula. S_\infty=\dfrac{a}{1-r}. It comes from the finite sum S_n=a\dfrac{1-r^n}{1-r} once r^n has vanished.
  • No limiting sum when |r|\ge 1. The terms do not die away, so the totals do not settle.
S_n=a\dfrac{1-r^n}{1-r}
S_\infty=\dfrac{a}{1-r}\qquad(|r|<1)

Worked mini-examples

Example · the frog

Question. The jumps are 4+2+1+\dfrac{1}{2}+\cdots. Show that the sum of the first n jumps is 8\left(1-\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^n\right), and find the limiting sum.

Formula: S_n=a\dfrac{1-r^n}{1-r} when r<1. If |r|<1, the limiting sum is S_\infty=\dfrac{a}{1-r}.

Step 1 · a=4. r=2\div 4=\dfrac{1}{2}.

Step 2 · S_n=4\times\dfrac{1-\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^n}{1-\dfrac{1}{2}}.

Step 3 · The bottom is \dfrac{1}{2}. Dividing by \dfrac{1}{2} is the same as multiplying by 2.

Step 4 · 4\times 2\left(1-\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^n\right)=8\left(1-\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^n\right).

Step 5 · \left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^n gets closer to 0 as n grows. So S_n gets closer to 8(1-0)=8.

Check · S_\infty=\dfrac{4}{1-\dfrac{1}{2}}=\dfrac{4}{\dfrac{1}{2}}=8.

Answer · The limiting sum is 8. The frog reaches the water.

Example · the limiting sum is given

Question. A GP has first term 10 and limiting sum 40. Find the ratio.

Formula: S_\infty=\dfrac{a}{1-r}. Here a=10 and S_\infty=40. The unknown is r.

Step 1 · \dfrac{10}{1-r}=40.

Step 2 · 10=40(1-r).

Step 3 · Divide by 10: 1=4(1-r).

Step 4 · 1=4-4r.

Step 5 · 4r=3, so r=\dfrac{3}{4}.

Check · \left|\dfrac{3}{4}\right|<1, so a limiting sum is allowed.

Example · no limiting sum

Question. Does 1+2+4+8+\cdots have a limiting sum?

Quick fact: A limiting sum exists only when |r|<1. If the ratio is 1 or bigger, or −1 or smaller, the terms do not shrink to 0.

Step 1 · a=1 and r=2\div 1=2.

Step 2 · |2|=2, which is bigger than 1.

Answer · There is no limiting sum. The terms keep doubling, so the totals keep growing.
