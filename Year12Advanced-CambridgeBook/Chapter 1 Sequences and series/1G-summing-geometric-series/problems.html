Exercise 1G

Problems with worked solutions

Attempt on paper first. Each part has its own solution button. Open one part at a time.

How to choose S_n. S_n means the sum of the first n terms. a is the first term. r is the number you multiply by to get the next term. If r>1, use S_n=\dfrac{a(r^n-1)}{r-1}. If r<1 (this includes every negative ratio), use S_n=\dfrac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}. If r=1, every term equals a, so S_n=na.

1 · Exercise 1G Foundation

Let S_6=2+6+18+54+162+486. Multiply by 3 to make 3S_6, then subtract S_6 in columns, and hence find S_6.

Quick fact: The multiplier is 3, because 6\div 2=3 and 18\div 6=3. Multiplying the whole sum by 3 shifts every term one place to the right. Subtraction then cancels the overlapping terms.

Step 1 · Write the six terms: S_6=2+6+18+54+162+486.

Step 2 · Multiply each term by 3. 2\times 3=6, 6\times 3=18, 18\times 3=54, 54\times 3=162, 162\times 3=486, 486\times 3=1458.

Step 3 · So 3S_6=6+18+54+162+486+1458.

Step 4 · Subtract in columns. The 6 cancels, the 18 cancels, the 54 cancels, the 162 cancels, and the 486 cancels. Left over: the last term of 3S_6 minus the first term of S_6.

Step 5 · 2S_6=1458-2=1456.

Step 6 · Divide by 2: S_6=1456\div 2=728.

Check · Add the six terms directly. 2+6=8, 8+18=26, 26+54=80, 80+162=242, 242+486=728.

S_6=728.

2 · Exercise 1G Foundation

(a) Use S_7=\dfrac{a(r^7-1)}{r-1} to find 1+3+3^2+3^3+3^4+3^5+3^6.

(b) Use S_7=\dfrac{a(1-r^7)}{1-r} to find 1-3+3^2-3^3+3^4-3^5+3^6.

Formula: The question tells us to use S_7=\dfrac{a(r^7-1)}{r-1}. Here a is the first term and r is the multiplier. This form fits because r>1.

Step 1 · The terms are 1,\;3,\;9,\;27,\;81,\;243,\;729. The first term is a=1. Each term is 3 times the one before, so r=3. There are 7 terms.

Step 2 · Build the power. 3^2=9, 3^3=27, 3^4=81, 3^5=243, 3^6=729, 3^7=2187.

Step 3 · Substitute. S_7=\dfrac{1\times(2187-1)}{3-1}.

Step 4 · 2187-1=2186 and 3-1=2, so S_7=\dfrac{2186}{2}.

Step 5 · 2186\div 2=1093.

S_7=1093.

Formula: The question tells us to use S_7=\dfrac{a(1-r^7)}{1-r}. Here r=-3, and -3<1, so this is the form we use.

Step 1 · The signs alternate, starting positive: 1,\;-3,\;9,\;-27,\;81,\;-243,\;729. First term a=1. The multiplier is r=-3, because -3\div 1=-3. There are 7 terms.

Step 2 · An odd power of a negative number stays negative. (-3)^7=-(3^7)=-2187.

Step 3 · Top: 1-(-2187)=1+2187=2188.

Step 4 · Bottom: 1-(-3)=1+3=4.

Step 5 · S_7=\dfrac{1\times 2188}{4}=547.

S_7=547.

3 · Exercise 1G Foundation

On the way to St Ives a speaker meets one man. The man has 7 wives, each wife has 7 sacks, each sack has 7 cats, and each cat has 7 kits. Only the speaker is going to St Ives. How many (man, wives, sacks, cats and kits) are going the other way?

Formula: These five counts form a GP with a=1 and r=7>1, so S_n=\dfrac{a(r^n-1)}{r-1}. The speaker is not in the sum.

Step 1 · Man: 1=7^0.

Step 2 · Wives: 7.

Step 3 · Sacks: 7\times 7=49.

Step 4 · Cats: 49\times 7=343.

Step 5 · Kits: 343\times 7=2401.

Step 6 · There are 5 groups, so n=5, a=1, r=7. Build 7^2=49, 7^3=343, 7^4=2401, 7^5=16807.

Step 7 · S_5=\dfrac{1\times(16807-1)}{7-1}=\dfrac{16806}{6}.

Step 8 · 16806\div 6=2801.

Check · 1+7=8, 8+49=57, 57+343=400, 400+2401=2801.

2801 were going the other way.

7 · Exercise 1G Development

A king puts 1 grain of wheat on the first square of a 64-square chessboard, 2 grains on the next square, 4 on the next, and so on, doubling each time.

(a) How many grains are on:

(i) the last square,

(ii) the whole chessboard?

(b) About 30 000 grains fill 1 litre. How many cubic kilometres of wheat are on the chessboard?

Formula: In a GP the nth term is a_n=ar^{n-1}. Here a is the first term and r is the multiplier.

Step 1 · Square 1 has 1=2^0 grain. Square 2 has 2=2^1. Square 3 has 4=2^2.

Step 2 · The power is one less than the square number. Square 64 has 2^{63} grains.

The last square has 2^{63} grains.

Formula: a=1 and r=2>1, so S_n=\dfrac{a(r^n-1)}{r-1}. The chessboard has 64 squares, so n=64.

Step 1 · S_{64}=\dfrac{1\times(2^{64}-1)}{2-1}.

Step 2 · The bottom is 1, so S_{64}=2^{64}-1.

The whole board has 2^{64}-1 grains.

Quick fact: 1\,\mathrm{km}=10^3\,\mathrm{m}, so 1\,\mathrm{km}^3=10^9\,\mathrm{m}^3. One litre is 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}^3. So 1\,\mathrm{km}^3=10^{12} litres, and 1 litre =10^{-12}\,\mathrm{km}^3.

Step 1 · Number of litres =\dfrac{2^{64}-1}{30000}.

Step 2 · 2^{64}=18446744073709551616, so 2^{64}-1=18446744073709551615.

Step 3 · Divide by 30 000: 614891469123651.7 litres, which is 6.14891\ldots\times 10^{14} litres.

Step 4 · To four significant figures that is 6.149\times 10^{14} litres. The digit after 8 is 9, so 8 rounds up.

Step 5 · Multiply by 10^{-12}: 6.14891\ldots\times 10^{14}\times 10^{-12}=614.891\ldots cubic kilometres.

Step 6 · To the nearest cubic kilometre, 614.891 rounds to 615.

About 615\,\mathrm{km}^3 of wheat.

8 · Exercise 1G Development

Find S_n and S_{10}. Clear any square root from the denominator.

(a) 1+\sqrt{2}+2+\cdots

(b) 2-2\sqrt{5}+10-\cdots

Formula: r=\sqrt{2}>1, so S_n=\dfrac{a(r^n-1)}{r-1}. To clear a square root from the bottom, multiply top and bottom by the conjugate \sqrt{2}+1.

Step 1 · a=1. \sqrt{2}\div 1=\sqrt{2}, and 2\div\sqrt{2}=\sqrt{2}, so r=\sqrt{2}.

Step 5 · n>\log_3\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\times 10^{20}+1\right)\approx 41.55.

Step 6 · The smallest whole n above 41.55 is 42.
